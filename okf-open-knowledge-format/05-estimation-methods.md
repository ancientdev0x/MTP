1. Comparison Between Two Types of Multifractal Modeling.pdf

Metadata: Qiuming Cheng and F. P. Agterberg, “Comparison Between Two Types of Multifractal Modeling,” Mathematical Geology 28(8), 1996, pp. 1001–1015. PDF metadata title: “Comparison between two types of multifractal modeling.” Authors are not populated in the PDF metadata. Local PDF: 15 pages. [PDF pp. 1–15]

Signal/data context: This is a theoretical and synthetic multifractal-measure paper, not a well-log study. The authors compare the Evertsz–Mandelbrot multifractal-spectrum formulation with the Schertzer–Lovejoy codimension formulation. Applications discussed in the introduction concern spatial geochemical concentrations, point processes, fractures, seismic epicentres and related geological spatial data, but the paper’s worked example is a binomial multiplicative cascade. [PDF pp. 1–2, 12–14]

Methods and equations: For cells of scale epsilon, the coarse Hölder exponent is defined as alpha_i approximately equal to log(mu_i(epsilon))/log(epsilon), and the multifractal spectrum is f(alpha) = lim log N_epsilon(alpha)/log(epsilon). The associated codimension-like function is c(alpha) = lim log Pr_epsilon(alpha)/log(epsilon), with c(alpha) = D - f(alpha). [PDF pp. 2–3]

The partition function is X_q(epsilon) = sum_i mu_i(epsilon)^q, with X_q(epsilon) proportional to epsilon^{tau(q)}. The minimizing exponent satisfies tau(q) = min_alpha[q alpha - f(alpha)], alpha(q) = d tau(q)/dq, and f(alpha) = q alpha - tau(q), i.e. the Legendre-transform construction. q = 0 gives the box-counting dimension of the support; q = 1 gives the information/entropy dimension under the stated normalization. [PDF pp. 3–6]

The Schertzer–Lovejoy formulation uses a scale ratio lambda = L/epsilon, field order gamma, codimension C(gamma), and moment scaling K(q). The source gives K(q) = max_gamma[q gamma - C(gamma)] and C(gamma) = max_q[q gamma - K(q)]. It derives gamma = D - alpha and relationships linking K(q), tau(q), f(alpha), c(alpha), and C(gamma). [PDF pp. 6–8]

Parameter choices and worked example: The binomial multiplicative cascade is built on the unit interval, repeatedly split into two equal parts, with random allocation controlled by B = plus or minus d. The illustration uses d = 0.4, equivalent to a 70:30 split at each cut. For the codimension calculation, seven scale values k = 4 through 10 are used; for the partition-function calculation, eight block sizes k = 3 through 10 are used. A parabolic ordinary-least-squares fit is applied to C(gamma) over approximately gamma = -0.09 to 0.49. Central differences over successive tau(q) values estimate alpha(q). Logarithmic plots use base 10. [PDF pp. 11–13]

Findings—source claims: The f(alpha) spectrum and the c(alpha) function characterize the same multifractal measure. The Schertzer–Lovejoy C(gamma) function agrees with the f(alpha)-based formulation only over gamma in the range D - alpha(0) <= gamma <= D - alpha_min. For gamma below D - alpha(0), C(gamma) becomes constant while f(alpha) remains continuous. Therefore f(alpha) contains more information, especially for asymmetric multifractals. The binomial-cascade experiment reproduces this relationship. [PDF pp. 1, 8, 12–14]

Limitations—source claims: The equivalence of the two models is restricted to a subset of field orders; use of the Schertzer–Lovejoy universal function requires restricting gamma to the valid range. The worked example is an idealized binomial cascade and does not test finite, noisy, irregularly sampled, nonstationary or instrument-processed signals. [PDF pp. 8, 13–14]

Relevance to well-log analysis—interpretation: The partition-function/Legendre-transform framework is directly relevant to estimating multifractal spectra from a well-log curve or from derived positive measures, increments, wavelet coefficients or scale-dependent distributions. The paper supports reporting f(alpha), tau(q), alpha(q), and the q-range explicitly rather than treating a single codimension curve as a complete description. It does not demonstrate the method on well logs; transfer to well logs is an interpretation requiring checks for finite-size effects, nonstationarity, missing data, tool resolution and the validity of a positive measure.

2. Four_methods_to_estimate_the_fractal_dimension_from_self-affine_signals_medical_application.pdf

Metadata: Hans E. Schepers, Johannes H. G. M. van Beek and James B. Bassingthwaighte, “Four Methods to Estimate the Fractal Dimension from Self-Affine Signals (Medical Application),” IEEE Engineering in Medicine and Biology Magazine, June 1992, pp. 57–64; DOI metadata identifies 10.1109/51.139038. PDF metadata lists the authors and journal citation. Local PDF: 8 pages. [PDF pp. 1–8]

Signal/data context: The article evaluates four one-dimensional methods using synthetic fractional Brownian motion/noise signals with known Hurst parameter H and fractal dimension D. It discusses medical time series conceptually but the numerical comparison uses generated signals. Fractional Brownian noise is obtained by successive differences of fractional Brownian motion. The two synthesis procedures are spectral synthesis (SSM) and successive random addition (SRA). [PDF pp. 1–3]

Core model and equations: For a one-dimensional self-affine signal, the source uses D = E + 1 - H = 2 - H, where E = 1. It classifies positive correlation as 1 < D < 1.5 and H > 0.5, random behavior as D = 1.5 and H = 0.5, and negative correlation as 1.5 < D < 2 and H < 0.5. [PDF pp. 1–2]

Method 1, relative-dispersion (RD) analysis: The signal is successively averaged over bins of size n; the relative dispersion is standard deviation divided by mean. The extracted equation is RD(n) = RD_0 n^(H-1), equivalently log(RD) = log(RD_0) + (H - 1) log(n). The equation text is partially corrupted in the PDF extraction, but the stated slope relationship and reported estimates support this form. RD is attractive for short spatial or temporal signals because geometrically increasing bin sizes require relatively few scale points. [PDF pp. 3–4]

Method 2, correlation analysis: The autocorrelation relation for fractional Brownian noise is given in terms of H and lag n. The extracted closed form is approximately r_n = 0.5[(n-1)^(2H) - 2n^(2H) + (n+1)^(2H)], although the typeset equation is partially damaged; this is flagged as equation-transcription uncertainty. The source fits the theoretical correlation curve and warns that including too many distant lags biases H toward 0.5 because few pairs remain and measurement noise dominates. [PDF pp. 4–5, 7]

Method 3, rescaled-range analysis: R/S is calculated over non-overlapping subintervals. The source uses R/S = c U^H, or log[R(U)/S(U)] = c + H log(U), with H estimated from the log–log slope. It notes that the result depends strongly on how the signal is partitioned, the selected minimum and maximum lags, and the number of subintervals. [PDF pp. 5–7]

Method 4, Fourier/spectral analysis: For fractional Brownian motion, the squared Fourier amplitude follows approximately |A(f)|^2 proportional to f^(-p), with p = 2H + 1. For fractional Brownian noise, differentiation reduces the spectral exponent by two, giving p = 2H - 1. H is obtained by fitting the slope of a log power-versus-frequency plot. The equation text is readable in context, but some superscripts are damaged in extraction; the stated relationships are explicit in the source. [PDF pp. 2, 6–7]

Parameter choices: Signals were generated with H values from 0.1 to 0.9 for method-comparison plots. Detailed tests used H = 0.75, ten trials per condition, signal lengths N = 512, 8192 and 32768, and both SSM and SRA synthesis. The paper illustrates an fBm with H = 0.75 and N = 8192. [PDF pp. 3, 5–8]

Findings—source claims: Spectral analysis generally gives the least biased estimates and the lowest variance among the four tested methods. RD performs well for sufficiently long signals and can work acceptably with shorter signals. Correlation estimates are biased toward H = 0.5 when too many distant coefficients are used. R/S estimates tend to be high and have large variability; their results depend on segmentation choices. The authors state that the methods are useful for one-dimensional spatial signals as well as time signals. [PDF pp. 7–8]

Limitations—source claims: Results depend on the signal-generation algorithm, finite signal length, selected lags and the assumptions of self-affinity. The four methods are not exhaustive; maximum-likelihood methods are mentioned as alternatives. The article warns that the estimated dimension of an embedded dynamical attractor is conceptually different from the graph dimension D between 1 and 2. [PDF pp. 1, 7–8]

Relevance to well-log analysis—interpretation: A well log is a 1-D spatial signal, so the four estimators are mathematically applicable in principle. Spectral slope and RD provide candidate monofractal diagnostics; R/S should be applied cautiously and preferably to increments if the raw log behaves like fBm, consistent with Li’s well-log study below. The paper does not analyze well logs, does not establish geological transferability, and does not address tool response, depth-domain irregularity, facies boundaries or nonstationarity typical of logs.

3. RS and Power spectral.pdf

Metadata: Chun-Feng Li, “Rescaled-range and power spectrum analyses on well-logging data,” Geophysical Journal International 153, 201–212, 2003. PDF metadata title: “Rescaled-range and power spectrum analyses on well-logging data”; metadata author: pinnacle, which conflicts with the article byline and is treated as unreliable. Local PDF: 12 pages. [PDF pp. 1–12]

Signal/data context: This is the only specified paper centered directly on well logs. It uses synthetic fractional Brownian motion/noise for validation and two real logs: (1) gamma-ray data sampled every 0.5 ft over 1984 ft from the Panoma gas field, Grant County, southwest Kansas, covering shale, sandstone, siltstone, gypsum, anhydrite and dolomite; and (2) sonic velocity data over 4249.5 ft, sampled every 0.5 ft, from Big Bow oil field, Stanton County, southwest Kansas, mainly interbedded carbonates and shale. [PDF pp. 2–3, 6, 8–11]

Methods and equations: R/S uses R/S = (a tau)^Hu, where R is the range of accumulated departures and S is the standard deviation. The power spectrum is S(f) proportional to f^(-beta), with beta = 2H + 1 for the global scaling exponent H. For fBm and its increments fGn, beta_fGn = beta_fBm - 2 and H_fGn approximately equals H_fBm - 1. The paper gives D approximately equal to 2 - Hu for fGn-like/increment data and D = 2 - H for fBm-like data. [PDF pp. 1–3]

The source distinguishes fBm, which is non-Gaussian and nonstationary, from fGn, its stationary successive-increment series. It argues that raw well logs are generally fBm-like because raw-log spectral powers are usually beta >= 1. Therefore, R/S should be applied to successive increments of the raw log, whereas the power spectrum can be fitted to the raw log over an appropriate wavelength range. [PDF pp. 2–3, 6, 11–12]

Preliminary simulation parameters: Twenty-one fBm series of 4096 points were generated with input H from 0 to 1 in increments of 0.05; fGn series were obtained by successive increments. The paper compares R/S on fBm, R/S on fGn, power spectrum, and variance-of-increments estimates. It reports an empirical simulation regression H approximately 1.23Hu - 0.16, with agreement near H = Hu approximately 0.7. [PDF pp. 3–4]

Well-log preprocessing and parameter choices: For gamma ray, the authors compare raw data, time-varying-variance normalization, moving-average trend removal with a 100-ft window, and successive increments. For sonic data, the moving-average removal window is 200 ft. The R/S analysis identifies transient zones; reported gamma-ray transient lengths are 70 ft for normalized data, 94.6 ft after moving-average removal and 9.3 ft for increments. Sonic transient lengths are reported as 212.7 ft, 170.3 ft and 11.2 ft for normalized, moving-average and increment data, respectively; the figure/table presentation has a minor discrepancy for the moving-average sonic value, so the tabulated value should be checked against the original plot if used quantitatively. [PDF pp. 6–10]

Findings—source claims: For simulated data, R/S applied directly to fBm gives Hu greater than approximately 0.85 and is not comparable with H from the spectrum. Applying R/S to fGn increments gives estimates close to the power-spectrum H. For the gamma-ray log, raw R/S gives Hu = 0.91, normalization Hu = 0.38, moving-average removal Hu = 0.54 and increments Hu = 0.52; the raw spectrum gives H = 0.27. The increment series has the shortest transient zone and highest R/S regression correlation, R = 0.998. The gamma-ray spectrum reports beta = 1.54 and H = 0.27. [PDF pp. 3–4, 6–8]

For the sonic log, raw R/S gives Hu = 0.89, normalized Hu = 0.51, moving-average Hu = 0.30 and increment Hu = 0.25; the raw spectrum gives H = 0.017 with beta = 1.034. The source reports D from increments as 1.75 and D from raw-spectrum H as 1.983, closer to independently calculated box-counting and regularization dimensions than D from raw R/S. Table 2 reports gamma-ray box-counting D_b = 1.37 and regularization D_r = 1.65; sonic D_b = 1.60 and D_r = 1.94. [PDF pp. 9–11]

The paper links R/S transient-zone lengths to the shortest wavelengths that can be included in a reliable long-wavelength spectral regression. It also reports a logging-tool moving-average effect that causes high-wavenumber spectral roll-off. The authors conclude that the spectrum method is generally less problematic than R/S for these logs, while increments are the most objective and stable input for R/S. [PDF pp. 7–12]

Limitations—source claims: Well logs are non-Gaussian, nonstationary and intermittent; normalization and trend removal can change R/S results substantially. Logging-tool resolution produces spectral roll-off. A high regression correlation does not guarantee a valid R/S slope: the sonic normalized series gives R = 0.99 but Hu = 0.75, which is too high relative to H = 0.017. Both methods require sufficient data points, and beta < 1 can occur spuriously when the sample is too short. At least two independent methods are recommended. [PDF pp. 6–12]

Relevance to well-log analysis—source-supported and interpretation: This paper directly supports a workflow for monofractal well-log analysis: inspect the raw-log spectrum and beta; classify the log as fBm-like or fGn-like; use increments for R/S if fBm-like; identify transient zones; exclude tool-resolution roll-off and non-scaling ranges from regression; compare independent estimates; and report the exact preprocessing. It does not establish that every well log is fBm-like or monofractal. Its recommendations are based on two logs plus simulations and should be tested across logging tools, formations, sampling intervals, facies and wells.

4. Use of Spectral method for the estimation of the Fractal Dimension.pdf

Metadata: S. E. Hough, “On the Use of Spectral Methods for the Determination of Fractal Dimension,” Geophysical Research Letters 16, 673–676, 1989. PDF metadata title: “On the use of spectral methods for the determination of fractal dimension”; author metadata: S. E. Hough. Local PDF: 4 pages. [PDF pp. 1–4]

Signal/data context: The paper concerns one-dimensional profiles of two-dimensional surfaces, particularly topographic and bathymetric profiles, while explicitly stating that the results apply to any 1-D profile. It discusses theoretical spectra, synthetic sine-wave constructions, concatenated profiles and examples from seafloor topography. It is not a well-log study. [PDF pp. 1–4]

Methods and equations: The finite-length Fourier transform is Z(f,T) = (1/T) integral_0^T z(t) exp(-2 pi i f t) dt, with spectral density S(f) = T|Z(f,T)|^2 as T tends to infinity. The two-point autocorrelation is G(tau) = <z(t)z(t+tau)> - <z(t)>^2, and the mean-square increment is M_2(tau) = <|z(t+tau)-z(t)|^2> = 2[<z^2> - <z>^2] - 2G(tau). [PDF p. 1]

For a 2-D surface profile with S(f) proportional to f^(-beta), the commonly used relationship is D = (5 - beta)/2, equivalently beta = 5 - 2D; combining with beta = 2H + 1 gives D = 2 - H. Hough emphasizes that the relationship is conditional and approximate, not a sufficient test for fractality. [PDF pp. 1–2]

Parameter/diagnostic choices: The synthetic example constructs sine-wave series with different spectral decays and concatenates three segments. The relevant frequency range in the plotted examples is approximately 0.01–0.1 Hz. A composite series can show an apparent 1/f^2 spectrum, corresponding to D = 1.5, even though its constituent segments have different spectral slopes. The paper recommends moving/split-window spectral analysis to identify statistically homogeneous segments; exact window lengths are not prescribed in the source. [PDF pp. 1, 3–4]

Findings—source claims: A power-law spectral density is necessary but not sufficient to identify a fractal distribution. Fourier power removes phase information, so random phase must be independently demonstrated before using spectral slope as a fractal-dimension estimator. Extending the Wiener–Khinchin relation to nonstationary signals creates additional problems. Concatenating or averaging segments with different spectral exponents can produce an apparent beta = 2 and D = 1.5. For beta > 3, the profile is differentiable and the graph dimension is D = 1 rather than a value below 1. [PDF pp. 1–4]

Limitations—source claims: The spectral-to-dimension formula can fail for nonstationary, non-random-phase, concatenated or differentiable signals. Instrument/data processing can alter spectral behavior. Long composite profiles may hide distinct statistically homogeneous provinces. The author concludes that careful analysis of limited homogeneous regions is required before inferring geological processes. [PDF pp. 1, 3–4]

Relevance to well-log analysis—interpretation: The warnings are directly applicable to logs: a log-wide beta near 2 or an estimated D near 1.5 may reflect concatenated facies, changing regimes, filtering or averaging rather than a single fractal process. Spectral estimates should therefore be computed in moving or facies-aware windows, with explicit frequency/wavelength ranges and tests for crossovers and phase/nonstationarity. The paper supports spectral screening but does not demonstrate a well-log application.

5. wavelet leadermultifractal spectra.pdf

Metadata: E. Serrano and A. Figliola, “Wavelet Leaders: A New Method to Estimate the Multifractal Singularity Spectra,” Physica A 388, 2793–2805, 2009, DOI 10.1016/j.physa.2009.03.043. The local PDF has no reliable populated document metadata but contains the article title, authors, journal, dates and DOI. Local PDF: 13 pages.

Signal/data context: The paper compares Wavelet Leaders (WL) with Multifractal Detrended Fluctuation Analysis (MFDFA) using Cantor measures, binomial multiplicative cascades and EEG signals from tonic–clonic epileptic seizures. The EEG is from a female patient with secondary generalized tonic–clonic epilepsy; scalp electrodes use the international 10–20 system, sampling is 409.6 Hz with 12-bit A/D conversion, an eight-pole low-pass Bessel anti-alias filter with 50-Hz cutoff, followed by a 1–50-Hz digital bandpass; the C4 derivation and first tonic seizure period are analyzed. Each natural series has 4096 samples. [PDF pp. 1, 10–12]

Multifractal formalism: For a pointwise Hölder exponent H_f(x_0), the source defines H_f(x_0) as the supremum of alpha for which a local polynomial approximation has error bounded by C|x-x_0|^alpha. The singularity spectrum d_f(H) is the Hausdorff dimension of points with exponent H. The generic scaling function is S(q,j) = r_j times the sum of mu(MI_jk)^q, with eta(q) = inf_H[1 - d_f(H) + Hq] and d_f(H) = inf_q[1 - eta(q) + Hq]. [PDF pp. 2–3]

MFDFA method: The signal profile is Y(k) = sum_{i=1}^k[x(i)-mean(x)]. It is divided into segments of length r; a polynomial p_nu(i) of order m is fitted and subtracted; the q-order fluctuation function F_q(r) is calculated across segments. The scaling law is F_q(r) proportional to r^{h(q)}. The source gives eta(q) = qh(q) - 1 and d_f(H) = inf_q[1 - qh(q) + Hq]. Positive q emphasizes large fluctuations; negative q emphasizes small fluctuations. [PDF pp. 4–5]

Wavelet and Wavelet Leaders methods: Standard wavelet coefficients c_jk define a structure function S_wc(q,j) = 2^{-j} sum_k |c_jk|^q, but ordinary wavelet formulations can be unstable for oscillating singularities and negative q. For a dyadic interval I_jk, the leader is d_jk = sup{|c_lh|: I_lh is contained in the dilated interval 3I_jk}, with the source allowing another dilation factor M >= 3. The leader scaling function is S_wl(q,j) = 2^{-j} sum_k d_jk^q, eta_wl(q) = lim inf log S_wl(q,j)/log(2^j), and d_f(H) = inf_q[1 - eta_wl(q) + Hq]. [PDF pp. 5–6]

Parameter choices: Theoretical Cantor examples use p1 = 0.60 and p2 = 0.40; binomial cascade examples use p1 = 0.25 and p2 = 0.75. Numerical series have 4096 points. The illustrative Cantor construction uses triadic intervals and Haar wavelets; the broader experiments use an orthogonal spline wavelet discrete transform. The source defines MFDFA polynomial order m but does not give a single universal order in the extracted text. The leader construction is dyadic and uses a dilation of at least three intervals. [PDF pp. 7–10]

Findings—source claims: For Cantor measures and binomial cascades, WL estimates agree more closely with the analytical spectrum than MFDFA, particularly for negative q and sparse positive measures. MFDFA becomes unstable when fluctuation functions are close to zero for q < 0. WL is more robust for sparse measures but requires a careful criterion for selecting significant leaders. For the three EEG series, both methods are broadly effective; WL estimates a wider Hölder-exponent range, while H* at maximum spectrum dimension is similar between methods. Reported EEG values are: WL1 H_min = 0.010, H_max = 0.778, Delta H = 0.768, H* = 0.272; MFDFA1 H_min = 0.096, H_max = 0.600, Delta H = 0.505, H* = 0.341; WL2 H_min = 0.010, H_max = 0.791, Delta H = 0.781, H* = 0.280; MFDFA2 H_min = 0.076, H_max = 0.668, Delta H = 0.591, H* = 0.304; WL3 H_min = 0.010, H_max = 0.837, Delta H = 0.872, H* = 0.301; MFDFA3 H_min = 0.083, H_max = 0.672, Delta H = 0.590, H* = 0.304. [PDF pp. 10–12]

Computational findings: The source estimates WL complexity as O(N log N) using the Mallat scheme, versus an upper-bound MFDFA complexity of approximately O(N^2), dependent on window lengths and polynomial fitting. It concludes that WL generally performs better, while MFDFA remains comprehensible and suitable for natural, weakly stationary, relatively short series. [PDF pp. 6, 12–13]

Limitations—source claims: The formalism assumes irregular signals with scale-self-similar or statistically self-similar structure and relies on stable scaling functions and Legendre transforms. WL still requires a significant-leader selection rule, and practical results are not completely independent of the wavelet basis. WL needs a reasonably large number of scales and therefore a relatively large number of samples. MFDFA can be unstable for sparse support and negative q. [PDF pp. 2–3, 7, 12–13]

Relevance to well-log analysis—interpretation: WL is a plausible method for multifractal characterization of 1-D well logs because it is explicitly designed for local Hölder regularity, multiscale coefficients and irregular signals. It could support facies-window comparison, heterogeneity metrics, scale-dependent roughness and multifractal spectra of logs or increments. However, the paper does not test well logs. A well-log implementation would need sensitivity tests for wavelet family, vanishing moments, leader dilation, scale range, q-range, depth sampling, tool filtering, facies boundaries, missing values and nonstationarity. The EEG and synthetic results demonstrate method behavior, not geological or reservoir transferability.

Cross-paper synthesis for the MTP topic: The sources collectively support separating three questions: (i) monofractal scaling from a spectral slope or Hurst-like exponent; (ii) multifractal scaling from tau(q), f(alpha), eta(q), or d_f(H); and (iii) whether the observed scaling is genuine rather than caused by concatenated regimes, trends, filtering, finite length or phase effects. For well logs, Li’s paper provides the most direct preprocessing guidance: inspect tool-induced roll-off and nonstationarity, use increments for R/S when the raw log is fBm-like, and validate with an independent spectral or variance-based method. Hough’s paper cautions that a power-law spectrum alone is insufficient. Cheng and Agterberg provide the multifractal-spectrum/codimension theory, while Serrano and Figliola provide an implementable WL-versus-MFDFA framework. None of the non-well-log papers demonstrates transferability to well logs; such transfer should be presented as a proposed methodology, not as a source-established result.
