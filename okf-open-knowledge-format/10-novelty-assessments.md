# Targeted Novelty Assessments

These are cautious web-search assessments, not proof of priority. Each was based on at least three targeted search rounds and the local literature notes.

## Sampling/tool stability

Decision: POTENTIALLY NOVEL, with low-to-moderate confidence. The methodology is not wholly novel: individual components—Hurst/R-S, spectral beta, variograms, DFA/MFDFA, wavelets, and synthetic fBm/fGn validation—are established. Application novelty is also limited because fractal and multifractal well-log analysis has substantial prior art. The potentially novel contribution is an integrated, reproducible robustness benchmark that treats sampling interval, smoothing, tool-response degradation, and preprocessing as controlled perturbations; compares H, spectral beta, variogram dimension, and R/S jointly; measures recovery error against synthetic known signals; and demonstrates stability or failure on public multi-tool well logs. I found no single paper in these searches that performs this exact end-to-end study, but absence of a retrieved paper is not proof of priority.

Search round 1 — direct well-log fractal/multifractal literature. Queries: (1) "well log fractal analysis Hurst spectral exponent variogram rescaled range sampling interval"; (2) "well-logging" fractal dimension spectral power R/S analysis; (3) multifractal analysis well logs gamma ray wavelet leaders MFDFA. Closest results: (a) Pang & North, “Fractals and Their Applicability in Geological Wireline Log Analysis” (1996), https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1747-5457.1996.tb00438.x — applies R/S to wireline logs and explicitly finds that changes in calculation procedure can alter a single-well dimension more than differences between wells; strong overlap in the warning that estimator settings matter, but no controlled sampling/tool-degradation benchmark. (b) Li, “Rescaled-range and power spectrum analyses on well-logging data” (2003), https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-246X.2003.01893.x — directly compares R/S and power spectra on gamma-ray and sonic logs, studies normalization, moving-average removal, increments, transient zones, and logging-tool spectral roll-off; very close overlap for H, beta, preprocessing, and tool effects, but not a systematic multi-estimator perturbation/recovery study. (c) Dolan, Bean & Riollet, “The broad-band fractal nature of heterogeneity in the upper crust from petrophysical logs” (1998), https://academic.oup.com/gji/article/132/3/489/576974 — evaluates autocorrelation, semivariogram, R/S, and power spectra on synthetic signals and multiple sonic/resistivity/petrophysical logs; strong overlap in cross-method comparison, synthetic validation, and multiple tool types, but the aim is crustal heterogeneity rather than stability across sampling, smoothing, and modeled response degradation. (d) Subhakar & Chandrasekhar, “Reservoir characterization using multifractal detrended fluctuation analysis of geophysical well-log data” (2016), https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physa.2015.10.103 — applies DFA/MFDFA to gamma-ray and neutron-porosity logs, uses windowed scaling, surrogate/shuffled data, and synthetic gas-corrected logs; strong multifractal/application overlap, but not the proposed robustness matrix or comparison with variogram/R-S/spectral beta under controlled degradation.

Search round 2 — sampling, smoothing, and tool-response effects. Queries: (1) well log sampling interval effect fractal dimension Hurst exponent; (2) well logging tool resolution spectral roll-off smoothing response frequency; (3) "Fractal Correction of Well Logging Curves" 2001. Closest results: (a) Matias-Gutierres et al., “Fractal Scaling of Storage Capacity Fluctuations in Well Logs from Southeastern Mexican Reservoirs” (2025), https://doi.org/10.3390/fractalfract9080548 — explicitly analyzes a family of local Hurst exponents at different sampling/observation intervals across five wells; this is the closest direct overlap with sampling-interval sensitivity, but it uses a Family–Vicsek/structure-function framework and does not benchmark H, spectral beta, variogram dimension, and R/S jointly or model tool-response degradation. (b) Li & Cheng, “Fractal Correction of Well Logging Curves” (2001), cited locally and searchable by title — models scale-dependent logging-curve correction for probe averaging, adjacent beds, borehole effects, and measurement error; close overlap with tool-response/preprocessing motivation, but it proposes correction rather than quantifying estimator-feature stability or known-signal recovery. (c) “Well logging super-resolution based on fractal interpolation enhanced by BiLSTM-AMPSO” (2025), https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40948-025-00969-9 — creates low-resolution data by 4× downsampling and evaluates recovery of high-frequency well-log structure; overlap with downsampling and resolution degradation, but it is a super-resolution method, not a fractal-feature stability assessment. (d) “Well log data super-resolution based on locally linear …” (2021), https://ogst.ifpenergiesnouvelles.fr/articles/ogst/full_html/2021/01/ogst210044/ogst210044.html — addresses high/low-resolution well-log relationships in the frequency domain; relevant to degradation and spectral effects, but not fractal estimator robustness.

Search round 3 — estimator robustness and synthetic recovery outside well logs. Queries: (1) Hurst exponent estimator robustness finite sample smoothing aggregation downsampling synthetic fractional Brownian motion comparison; (2) multifractal well log MFDFA wavelet multifractal spectrum sampling interval depth resolution; (3) fractal dimension estimation self-affine signals effect of smoothing sampling frequency variogram spectral R/S. Closest results: (a) Schepers, van Beek & Bassingthwaighte, “Four Methods to Estimate the Fractal Dimension from Self-Affine Signals” (1992), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3459993 — compares relative dispersion, correlation, R/S, and Fourier methods on known fBm/fGn signals and documents bias, variance, finite-length, lag, and segmentation effects; very strong methodological precedent, but not well-log data, smoothing, tool response, or downsampling as a logging workflow. (b) Krakovská & Krakovská, “Fractal Dimension of Self-Affine Signals: Four Methods of Estimation” (2016), arXiv:1611.06190, https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.06190 — tests Higuchi, spectral decay, generalized Hurst, and DFA on Weierstrass/fBm signals with known dimensions, noise, and data-length variation; overlap with controlled synthetic benchmarking, but not well logs or tool physics and not the proposed estimator set. (c) Bassingthwaighte & Raymond, “Evaluating scaled windowed variance methods for estimating the Hurst coefficient of time series” (2011), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3204962 — quantifies bias/standard deviation versus series length and added noise; supports the need for error-based stability analysis, but does not address logging-specific perturbations. (d) Olivares et al., “Wavelet-based discrimination of isolated singularities masquerading as multifractals in detrended fluctuation analyses” (2021), https://doi.org/10.1007/s11071-020-05581-y — shows that isolated singularities can produce spurious broad multifractal spectra in MFDFA and wavelet leaders; important overlap for preprocessing/artifact controls, but no well-log or sampling/tool-response study.

What is already known: fractal dimensions and Hurst/spectral parameters have been repeatedly extracted from wireline logs; R/S and power-spectrum estimates can disagree substantially; moving-average detrending, increments, finite length, window choices, nonstationarity, and tool resolution can change results; multifractal well-log studies already use DFA/MFDFA, wavelets, shuffled/surrogate tests, and synthetic corrections; multi-tool comparisons and synthetic self-affine benchmarks exist separately; and at least one 2025 well-log paper explicitly varies sampling/observation intervals for local Hurst analysis.

What appears to remain: a single controlled protocol quantifying feature stability and estimator error across a factorial perturbation design: original versus downsampled logs, multiple anti-aliasing/smoothing choices, explicit convolution with tool-response kernels or resolution degradation, alternative normalization/detrending/increment preprocessing, and multiple estimators including H, spectral beta, variogram dimension, and R/S. A strong gap is also the absence, in the retrieved literature, of recovery metrics tied to known synthetic signals plus public demonstrations across logging tools, with uncertainty intervals, crossover detection, and explicit separation of true geological scaling from artifacts. The work should be framed as a robustness/reproducibility and measurement-effects study, not as invention of a new fractal estimator.

Cautious positioning: methodology novelty is potentially meaningful if the perturbation matrix, tool-response model, synthetic ground truth, statistical stability criteria, and public reproducible implementation are genuinely comprehensive and reveal actionable estimator failure regimes. Application novelty alone—applying existing fractal or multifractal features to another well or reservoir—would be weak and largely not novel. The 2025 sampling-interval paper and the 2015 AGU abstract “Correlating Petrophysical Well Logs Using Fractal-based Analysis to Identify Changes in the Signal Complexity Across Neutron, Density, Dipole Sonic, and Gamma Ray Tool Types,” https://agu.confex.com/agu/fm15/webprogram/Paper78461.html, make claims close to sampling/tool-type stability, so the proposed study must distinguish itself through controlled degradation, cross-estimator agreement/error analysis, and ground-truth recovery. Search limitations: some publisher pages and Semantic Scholar/OpenAlex-style backends were unavailable or rate-limited; conclusions are based on three targeted web-search rounds, retrieved abstracts/full-text pages, and the supplied local literature notes, not an exhaustive systematic review.

## Surrogate-controlled genuine multifractality

Three targeted web-search rounds were completed, supplemented by the local MTP literature notes. Round 1 queries: (1) "well logs" multifractality surrogate testing facies, (2) "well log" "multifractal" "surrogate", (3) "false multifractality" nonstationarity facies, and (4) "well logs" multifractal nonstationary detrending. Closest results were: Dashtian, Jafari, Sahimi & Masihi, "Scaling, multifractality, and long-range correlations in well log data of large-scale porous media" (Physica A, 2011; https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378437111000732), which applies MF-DFA plus shuffled and surrogate logs and attributes multifractality to broad PDFs and long-range correlations; Shiri et al., "Multifractality nature and multifractal detrended fluctuation analysis of well logging data" (source-indexed as 2018/2020; PDF: https://www.internationalscholarsjournals.com/articles/multifractality-nature-and-multifractal-detrended-fluctuation-analysis-of-well-logging-data.pdf), which likewise compares original, shuffled and phase-randomized/surrogate RHOB, DT, AI and NPHI logs; and Hernandez-Martinez et al., "Facies Recognition Using Multifractal Hurst Analysis: Applications to Well-Log Data" (Mathematical Geosciences 45, 2013, 471–486, doi:10.1007/s11004-013-9445-6), which uses multifractal/Hurst behavior for electrofacies recognition. Round 2 queries: (1) "well-log" "surrogated" multifractal detrended fluctuation analysis, (2) "well log" "shuffled" "surrogate" multifractality source, (3) "facies" "multifractal" "well-log" segmentation wavelet DFA, and (4) "concatenated" "multifractal" nonstationary signals facies. Closest results were the same well-log surrogate studies above, plus Ouadfeul & Aliouane, "Automatic lithofacies segmentation using the Wavelet Transform Modulus Maxima lines combined with the Detrended Fluctuation Analysis" (doi:10.1007/s12517-011-0383-7), which explicitly segments lithofacies using WTMM/DFA, and Chandrasekhar et al., "Reservoir characterization using multifractal detrended fluctuation analysis of geophysical well-log data" (Physica A 445, 2016, 57–65, doi:10.1016/j.physa.2015.10.103), which relates MFDFA behavior to shale, sedimentation pattern and gas correction while discussing long-range correlation versus broad distributions. Round 3 queries: (1) "multifractal" "mixture" "nonstationarity" surrogate time series, (2) "false multifractality" well log, (3) "genuine multifractality" surrogate geological well log, (4) "tool response" well log fractal multifractal, and (5) "well log" facies adaptive scaling windows. The closest methodological results were Oswiecimka et al., "On the multifractal effects generated by monofractal signals" (arXiv:1307.2014; https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1307.2014), which quantifies finite-size false multifractality amplified by persistence and warns that nonstationarity, noise, periodicity and finite records can corrupt MF-DFA; and Leonarduzzi et al., "A Generalized Multifractal Formalism for the Estimation of Nonconcave Multifractal Spectra" (arXiv:1811.03463), which demonstrates that concatenating processes with different multifractal spectra can produce nonconcave spectra and suggests segmentation/nonstationarity diagnostics. Additional surrogate-testing precedent outside well logs includes IAAFT/phase-randomized surrogate tests for separating linear correlations, heavy tails and nonlinear multifractality, but the searches did not identify a paper applying that complete attribution design to facies-segmented well logs. Overlap: the candidate is not novel in its individual ingredients. Well-log multifractality, shuffled/phase-randomized surrogate comparisons, nonstationary-log cautions, facies/lithofacies segmentation, and local or moving-window scaling are all represented in prior work. The strongest direct overlap is with Dashtian et al. and Shiri et al., because they already use original, shuffled and surrogate well-log series to diagnose sources of apparent multifractality. Hernandez-Martinez et al., Ouadfeul & Aliouane, and Chandrasekhar et al. cover facies recognition, lithofacies segmentation, local scaling and geological interpretation. The false-multifractality literature establishes that finite length and persistence can create apparent multifractality even without genuine multiscaling. Remaining gap: I found no exact prior paper combining, in one reproducible well-log workflow, geological/facies segmentation before estimation; explicit comparison of whole-log versus within-facies estimates; detrending and scale-window selection tied to geological stationarity; tool-resolution/sampling and depth-domain controls; multiple nulls including shuffle and phase-randomized/IAAFT surrogates; and calibrated false-positive thresholds or uncertainty for declaring residual multifractality genuine. This gap is narrower than the candidate wording suggests: it is a workflow/validation contribution, not a new multifractal estimator or a first use of surrogates on well logs. Cautious novelty decision: not conceptually novel as currently phrased; low-to-medium novelty confidence for the broad idea, because the core surrogate-control and facies-aware components are already individually published. A potentially publishable MTP contribution could remain if it is explicitly framed as a well-log-specific, preregistered quality-control and attribution protocol, validated on synthetic regime mixtures plus held-out real wells, with quantitative null calibration, leakage-safe facies segmentation, tool/sampling sensitivity analysis, and a decision rule for rejecting apparent multifractality. The claim should be limited to “no exact combination was located in these searches,” not “the method is novel.”

## Uncertainty calibration

Literature-search assessment (4 targeted rounds; confidence in this assessment: medium, with residual uncertainty because several publisher pages were inaccessible and searches were web-index based rather than a complete database review).

Round 1 queries:
1. "well log fractal dimension uncertainty confidence interval bootstrap"
2. "multifractal well logging surrogate data finite sample bias"
3. "spectral Hurst exponent confidence intervals bootstrap geophysical time series"
4. "variogram fractal dimension well logs uncertainty"
Closest findings: Pang & North, “Fractals and Their Applicability in Geological Wireline Log Analysis” (Journal of Petroleum Geology, 1996; DOI: 10.1111/j.1747-5457.1996.tb00438.x) applied R/S fractal analysis to gamma-ray wireline logs and explicitly warned that procedural choices can change estimates more than between-well differences. Dashtian et al., “Scaling, multifractality, and long-range correlations in well log data of large-scale porous media” (Physica A, 2011; DOI: 10.1016/j.physa.2011.01.010) compared spectral density, multifractal random walk, and MFDFA on several well-log types and used shuffled/surrogate data to investigate sources of multifractality. Subhakar & Chandrasekhar, “Reservoir characterization using multifractal detrended fluctuation analysis of geophysical well-log data” (Physica A, 2016; DOI: 10.1016/j.physa.2015.10.103) used DFA/MFDFA on well logs, local windows, and synthetic gas-corrected logs for reservoir interpretation. Overlap is strong on well-log fractal/multifractal attributes, surrogate controls, nonstationarity-aware DFA, and comparison of estimation methods, but these sources do not establish a unified uncertainty-calibration framework across the proposed attribute families.

Round 2 queries:
1. "well log" "confidence interval" fractal
2. "well logging data" multifractal bootstrap
3. "finite sample" bias Hurst exponent estimator confidence interval
4. "bias" "rescaled-range" fractal dimension well log
Closest findings: Wendt, Roux & Abry, “Bootstrap for Log Wavelet Leaders Cumulant Based Multifractal Analysis” (EUSIPCO, 2006; PDF: https://eurasip.org/Proceedings/Eusipco/Eusipco2006/papers/1568980529.pdf) used synthetic multifractal processes and moving-block bootstrap to construct confidence intervals for log-cumulant multifractal estimates from one finite-length realization, with coverage evaluated by Monte Carlo simulation. Jach & Kokoszka, “Wavelet-Based Confidence Intervals for the Self-Similarity Parameter” (PDF indexed at https://www.stat.colostate.edu/~piotr/GSCS_A_258638.pdf) documented finite-sample boundary bias and poor coverage of nominal asymptotic intervals, and compared bootstrap/block-bootstrap alternatives. Weron, “Estimating long-range dependence: finite sample properties and confidence intervals” (the indexed abstract/search result for the finite-sample Hurst literature) compared R/S, DFA, DMA, and GHE under finite samples and heavy tails. These papers substantially overlap with confidence intervals, bootstrap, finite-sample bias, and surrogate/synthetic calibration, but are not a well-log study spanning spectral H, variogram dimension, R/S, and multifractal attributes under realistic logging artifacts.

Round 3 queries:
1. "fractal dimension estimator finite sample bias filtering missing data time series"
2. "multifractal analysis confidence intervals surrogate testing nonstationary signal"
3. "well log fractal multifractal robustness noise filtering scale range"
4. "uncertainty quantification Hurst exponent local nonstationary missing observations"
Closest findings: “Uncertainty in fractal dimension estimated from power spectra and variograms” (Mathematical Geosciences, 1997; DOI: 10.1007/BF02768900) is a particularly important methodological precedent: 36,000 simulated realizations showed bias in both spectral and variogram estimators, including overestimation/underestimation regimes, and identified effects of white noise and mixed fractal components. “Effect of the signal filtering on detrended fluctuation analysis” (Physica A, 2017; DOI indexed as S0378437117312566) reported that filtering changes the usable DFA scale range, differently for fractal Gaussian noise and fractional Brownian motion. Lancaster et al., “Surrogate data for hypothesis testing of physical systems” (Physics Reports, 2018) provides the broader surrogate-testing framework; the search also surfaced a 2022 study showing that stationary IAAFT surrogates can mis-handle nonstationary signals and that nonstationarity-preserving surrogates may be needed. These findings directly support treating filtering, nonstationarity, surrogate construction, and finite-sample bias as calibration factors rather than merely preprocessing details.

Round 4 queries:
1. "Uncertainty in fractal dimension estimated from power spectra and variograms"
2. "Bootstrap for log wavelet leaders cumulant based multifractal analysis"
3. "Reservoir characterization using multifractal detrended fluctuation analysis of geophysical well-log data"
4. "Fractals and their applicability in geological wireline log analysis"
Closest findings: Wendt, Abry & Jaffard, “Bootstrap for Empirical Multifractal Analysis” (IEEE Signal Processing Magazine, 2007; DOI: 10.1109/MSP.2007.4286563) formalized nonparametric bootstrap for multifractal confidence intervals and p-values. Wendt & Abry, “Multifractality Tests Using Bootstrapped Wavelet Leaders” (IEEE Transactions on Signal Processing, 2007; DOI: 10.1109/TSP.2007.896269) developed bootstrap-based multifractality testing. Leonarduzzi, Torres & Abry, “Scaling Range Automated Selection for Wavelet Leader Multifractal Analysis” (Signal Processing, 2014; DOI: 10.1016/j.sigpro.2014.06.002) used bootstrap to select scaling ranges and explicitly optimized the bias-variance trade-off under noise and departures from exact scaling. A recent 2025 paper, “Questioning Normality: A Study of Wavelet Leaders Distribution” (arXiv:2503.08821; subsequently indexed with DOI 10.1007/s11222-025-10767-8), revisits confidence intervals and distributional assumptions for wavelet-leader multifractal parameters, indicating that this methodological area remains active and that a simple bootstrap wrapper would not by itself be distinctive.

Most defensible overlap summary: Existing work already covers (i) fractal and multifractal analysis of well logs, including R/S, spectral, DFA/MFDFA, wavelet, and surrogate/shuffled controls; (ii) bootstrap confidence intervals and hypothesis tests for multifractal attributes; (iii) finite-sample bias in Hurst, spectral, variogram, and R/S-related estimators; (iv) filtering-induced scale-range changes; and (v) bootstrap-based scaling-range selection. Therefore, the broad claim “add uncertainty intervals to well-log fractal attributes” is not novel enough on its own.

A potentially distinct contribution would require a unified, explicitly calibrated uncertainty protocol for well-log use: jointly benchmarking spectral H, variogram dimension, R/S, and a carefully limited set of multifractal features; generating synthetic controls that reproduce well-log-relevant persistence, heavy tails, trends, layer boundaries, correlated measurement noise, filtering, gaps/missingness, and local nonstationarity; estimating both bias and interval coverage across finite lengths and scale ranges; using appropriate block/bootstrap or model-based/surrogate resampling rather than iid resampling; preserving nonstationarity in null surrogates where required; and validating calibration on held-out intervals or wells. The contribution should report empirical coverage, interval width, bias, false-positive rates for multifractality/persistence, and failure modes—not only point estimates or visually plausible spectra.

Cautious decision: conditionally promising but not established as novel. The search found no exact paper combining all proposed elements in a well-log setting, but that absence is not sufficient evidence of a gap because indexing and access were incomplete. The idea is likely publishable only if the methodological contribution is more than an application of existing bootstrap/wavelet-leader procedures: it should deliver a reproducible finite-sample calibration benchmark and demonstrate materially improved coverage or decision reliability under realistic well-log artifacts. Novelty confidence: medium-low for the broad concept; medium for the narrower contribution centered on cross-estimator calibration, artifact-aware surrogate design, and held-out well-interval validation.

## Cross-well generalization

Literature-search assessment (three targeted rounds)

Round 1 — fractal/Hurst features and cross-well relevance
Queries: (1) “fractal dimension Hurst exponent well logs facies classification cross well generalization”; (2) “fractal features well logging reservoir characterization transfer learning”; (3) “multifractal well log facies classification wavelet Hurst”.
Closest papers:
- Hernández-Martínez et al. (2013), “Facies Recognition Using Multifractal Hurst Analysis: Applications to Well-Log Data,” Mathematical Geosciences 45, 471–486, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11004-013-9445-6. Uses R/S-derived Hurst variations on well logs calibrated with core information to associate Hurst behavior with lithological/facies characteristics. It establishes the direct facies-recognition precedent for Hurst features, but does not establish a transfer-learning or held-out-well feature-transfer study from the retrieved evidence.
- López and Aldana (2007), “Facies recognition using wavelet based fractal analysis and waveform classifier at the Oritupano-A Field, Venezuela,” Nonlinear Processes in Geophysics 14, 325–335, DOI: https://doi.org/10.5194/npg-14-325-2007. Applies wavelet-based fractal analysis to sonic, density, gamma-ray, and porosity logs, relating slope, intercept, and fractal dimension to lithological content, followed by waveform classification. This is a close methodological antecedent, but it is a single-field recognition study rather than a cross-well generalization benchmark.
- Partovi and Sadeghnejad (2017), “Fractal parameters and well-logs investigation using automated well-to-well correlation,” Computers & Geosciences 103, 59–67, DOI located in search results as https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cageo.2017.03.024. Uses wavelet-derived fractal parameters (including wavelet exponent, wavelet standard-deviation exponent, and Hausdorff dimension) for automated well-to-well correlation in an Iranian oil field. This is the closest cross-well/fractal paper, but its objective is geological-boundary matching/correlation, not supervised facies or reservoir-class prediction with explicit transferability analysis.
- Mukherjee and Roy (2020), “Delineation of hydrocarbon and non-hydrocarbon zones using fractal analysis of well-log data from Bhogpara oil field, NE India,” Carbonates and Evaporites, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s13146-020-00556-x. Uses R/S and power-spectral-density Hurst estimates across five boreholes to distinguish hydrocarbon from non-hydrocarbon intervals. It supports multi-well use of fractal attributes, but the retrieved description does not show leakage-controlled predictive transfer evaluation.

Round 2 — Hurst/wavelet features for facies or reservoir classification
Queries: (1) “wavelet features well logs lithofacies classification Hurst exponent”; (2) “wavelet transform reservoir characterization well log classification”; (3) “Hurst exponent well log permeability lithofacies”.
Closest papers:
- López and Aldana (2007), above: direct wavelet-fractal facies-recognition precedent.
- Hernández-Martínez et al. (2013), above: direct multifractal-Hurst facies-recognition precedent.
- Tang et al. (2006), “Facies identification from well logs: A comparison of discriminant analysis and naïve Bayes classifier,” Computers & Geosciences, DOI: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0920410506001082. Uses conventional well-log predictors and evaluates rules on three holdout wells; it explicitly discusses distributional heterogeneity between physical sites and overfitting. It is a strong baseline/protocol precedent, but not a fractal-feature study.
- Tschannen, Rodriguez, and Keuper (2017), “Facies classification from well logs using an inception convolutional network,” arXiv:1706.00613, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1706.00613. Uses short depth windows and evaluates on blind wells from the Hugoton/Panoma dataset. It demonstrates that blind-well facies prediction is established and discusses overfitting/generalization limitations, but it learns convolutional representations rather than testing explicit Hurst/fractal attributes.
- ALAUDah et al. (2019), “A machine-learning benchmark for facies classification,” Interpretation, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1190/INT-2018-0249.1. Retrieved search evidence identifies it as a benchmark precedent for well-log facies classification. Its relevance is the comparative-baseline framing; the searched source was access-blocked, so details of its split protocol should be verified directly before relying on them.

Round 3 — leakage-controlled well-wise validation and transferability
Queries: (1) “well log machine learning leave-one-well-out validation data leakage”; (2) “reservoir facies classification cross-well validation machine learning”; (3) “grouped cross validation wells petrophysical machine learning leakage”.
Closest papers/resources:
- Tang et al. (2006), above: trains on wells and applies the learned facies-log relationship to three held-out wells; explicitly notes heterogeneity across physical sites and overfitting risks. This is an early, peer-reviewed well-wise validation precedent.
- Tschannen et al. (2017), above: uses nine wells for training and two blind wells for facies evaluation. It provides a direct blind-well generalization precedent, although not a formal leave-one-well-out sweep and not a leakage audit for feature selection.
- Fadokun et al. (2024), “Machine learning techniques for facies classification…” Energy Geoscience, DOI/PDF: https://www.sciopen.com/local/article_pdf/10.1016/j.engeos.2024.100353.pdf. Uses multiple training wells and an additional validation well with blind-well testing. It is useful as an application baseline, but the retrieved text reports conventional ML and does not establish fractal-feature transferability or a fully nested leakage-control protocol.
- “Splitting by Well, Not by Row: Leakage-Safe Evaluation for Petrophysical ML,” https://www.earthscan.io/insights/data-leakage-well-level-splits-petrophysical-ml. This is methodological guidance rather than a peer-reviewed paper; it states that all samples from a borehole must remain on one side of the split. Use it only as supporting protocol guidance, not as primary novelty evidence.
- A recent cross-well sonic-log study retrieved from Scientific Reports, “Cross-well machine learning prediction of sonic logs in Newfoundland and Labrador,” https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-36053-9, explicitly performs blind cross-well evaluation and confines feature selection, scaling, and tuning to the training well. It is not about facies or fractal features, but it is a close modern precedent for leakage-free transfer evaluation and for reporting asymmetric inter-well distribution shift.

Overlap with the candidate idea
1. Fractal, multifractal, Hurst, wavelet, Hausdorff, and related scaling attributes have already been used for facies recognition, lithological interpretation, hydrocarbon-zone discrimination, and well-to-well correlation.
2. Blind-well or holdout-well validation is already established in conventional and deep-learning well-log facies studies.
3. Well-wise/grouped splitting is not itself novel; the underlying rationale is known, and recent cross-well studies make leakage control explicit.
4. The literature is fragmented: fractal studies mostly demonstrate interpretive/classification utility within one field or formation; generalization studies mostly use raw logs, conventional statistics, or learned neural representations. I found no directly matching paper in the searched results that systematically tests whether selected fractal/Hurst/wavelet well-log features transfer across held-out wells or formations under a leakage-controlled, well-wise protocol while comparing them against conventional log statistics.

Remaining contribution
A defensible contribution would be an empirical transferability study, not a claim that Hurst or wavelet features are newly invented. The study should: (a) define fractal/Hurst/wavelet features before model fitting; (b) use leave-one-well-out or grouped outer validation; (c) perform imputation, normalization, feature selection, and hyperparameter tuning inside each training fold only; (d) compare fractal-only, conventional-statistics-only, raw-log, and combined feature sets; (e) report per-well and pooled macro-F1/balanced accuracy, confidence intervals or bootstrap uncertainty, and performance degradation relative to within-well/random splits; (f) test robustness across fields/formations if available; and (g) analyze whether apparent gains persist under changes in window size, wavelet family, Hurst estimator, depth sampling, and class imbalance. A particularly useful result could be a negative or conditional result showing that fractal attributes are field-specific rather than universally transferable.

Cautious novelty decision
Application novelty: MODERATE to HIGH, conditional on execution. Applying established fractal/Hurst/wavelet attributes to the explicit question of cross-well or cross-formation reservoir-characterization transferability appears insufficiently covered by the retrieved literature. The strongest novelty is the systematic comparison of transfer performance and failure modes, especially across formations, rather than the use of the attributes for classification itself.

Methodological novelty: LOW to MODERATE. Leave-one-well-out/grouped validation and leakage prevention are established practices, so they should be framed as rigor and a necessary evaluation design, not as a new validation algorithm. Methodological novelty could become moderate only if the work introduces a genuinely new transferability metric, domain-shift decomposition, fold-safe feature-selection framework tailored to fractal estimators, or a principled invariance/normalization method and demonstrates its value.

Overall decision: cautiously promising, but not confirmed novel. The most supportable claim is: “The study provides a leakage-controlled, well-wise benchmark of the cross-well transferability of fractal reservoir features and quantifies when they add value beyond conventional log statistics.” Avoid claiming the first use of fractal/Hurst/wavelet features for facies or reservoir characterization, or the first use of blind-well validation. Direct full-text verification of the Partovi–Sadeghnejad bibliographic DOI and the ALAUDah benchmark’s exact split/feature-selection protocol is still advisable before finalizing a novelty statement.

## Multifractal measure selection

Conducted 3 targeted literature-search rounds.

Round 1 — well-log multifractal measure definitions. Query set: (i) "well log multifractal analysis measure definition raw log absolute increments partition function positive measure", (ii) "generalized multifractal analysis geological formations well logs", and (iii) "well logs multifractal partition function texture measure". Closest papers: Khue, Huseby, Saucier & Muller (2002), “Application of generalized multifractal analysis for characterization of geological formations,” J. Phys.: Condens. Matter 14, 2347–2352, DOI 10.1088/0953-8984/14/9/323; and Ouadfeul & Aliouane (2011), “Multifractal analysis revisited by the continuous wavelet transform applied in lithofacies segmentation from well-logs data,” Int. J. Appl. Phys. Math. 1, 10–18, DOI 10.7763/IJAPM.2011.V1.3. Khue et al. construct texture logs from dipmeter microresistivity and use generalized dimensions, especially D1, as heterogeneity indices complementary to GR. Ouadfeul & Aliouane use CWT/WTMM and generalized dimensions for lithofacies boundaries. Overlap: explicit multifractal measures and well-log interpretation are established. Missing: no controlled comparison of alternative positive measures derived from the same raw well-log curve, and no reproducibility ranking across wells.

Round 2 — wavelet-leader/MFDFA well-log studies. Query set: (i) "well-log wavelet leaders multifractal", (ii) "well logging MFDFA original shuffled surrogate", and (iii) "well log multifractal WTMM MFDFA reservoir characterization". Closest papers: Subhakar & Chandrasekhar (2016), “Reservoir characterization using multifractal detrended fluctuation analysis of geophysical well-log data,” Physica A, DOI 10.1016/j.physa.2015.10.103; Ouadfeul & Aliouane (2013), “Automatic lithofacies segmentation using the wavelet transform modulus maxima lines combined with the detrended fluctuation analysis,” Arabian Journal of Geosciences 6, 625–634, DOI 10.1007/s12517-011-0383-7; and Gaci & Zaourar (2010), “Local regularity analysis of strata heterogeneities from sonic logs,” Nonlinear Processes in Geophysics 17, 455–466, DOI 10.5194/npg-17-455-2010. Subhakar & Chandrasekhar apply MFDFA to GR and neutron-porosity logs from two wells, compare original and gas-corrected neutron logs, and use shuffled/surrogate reasoning to attribute multifractality largely to long-range correlations. Ouadfeul & Aliouane combine WTMM with DFA for lithofacies segmentation. Gaci & Zaourar estimate local regularity from wavelet approaches and the increment process. The searches found well-log WTMM/CWT studies, but no clearly documented well-log study centered on modern discrete wavelet leaders as one member of a controlled measure-selection benchmark. Overlap: wavelet-based multifractality, MFDFA, well-log application, and surrogate tests each exist separately or in limited combinations. Missing: a matched comparison of wavelet-leader magnitudes versus MFDFA and positive partition measures under identical scale ranges, q orders, detrending, and boundary handling.

Round 3 — raw values versus increments/positive measures and methodological validity. Query set: (i) "multifractal measure absolute increments partition function signal", (ii) "positive measure multifractal raw signal increments", (iii) "apparent spurious multifractality absolute increments self-affine processes", and (iv) "singular measures versus structure functions geophysical fields". Closest papers: Davis, Marshak, Wiscombe & Cahalan (1994), “Multifractal characterizations of nonstationarity and intermittency in geophysical fields,” JGR 99, 8055–8072, NASA record 19950029691; Neuman (2010), “Apparent/spurious multifractality of data sampled from fractional Brownian/Lévy motions,” Hydrological Processes, DOI 10.1002/hyp.7611; and Neuman (2012), “Apparent multifractality and scale-dependent distribution of data sampled from self-affine processes,” Hydrological Processes, DOI 10.1002/hyp.7967. Davis et al. explicitly distinguish singular-measure analysis of a nonnegative field derived from data from structure functions of absolute increments. Neuman shows that positive square/absolute increments of finite samples from self-affine fBm/fLm can display apparent or spurious multifractality. Overlap: the proposed raw-mass/absolute-increment distinction has strong methodological precedent outside well logs. Missing: these warnings have not, in the located literature, been operationalized as a well-log measure-selection protocol with cross-well stability and null-model validation.

Additional relevant comparison: Wang et al. (2019), “Fractal and Multifractal Properties of Electrographic Signals,” Frontiers in Physiology, DOI/PMC article PMC6295567, compares MF-DFA, MF-DMA, and Chhabra–Jensen, notes that Chhabra–Jensen requires a positive measure, applies a sigmoid transformation to signed data, and tests stability against variance and surrogates. This is not a well-log study, but it demonstrates that the positivity transformation itself can materially affect the estimated spectrum; therefore a transformed-property measure cannot be treated as an innocuous preprocessing choice.

Overlap synthesis: Existing well-log work covers (a) generalized dimensions/texture measures, (b) WTMM/CWT singularity analysis, (c) MFDFA, (d) original-versus-corrected logs, and (e) shuffled/surrogate attribution tests. General multifractal literature covers the conceptual distinction between nonnegative measures and absolute-increment structure functions and documents spurious multifractality risks. No located paper combines these into a factorial, same-data test of raw positive mass, absolute increments, transformed property, and wavelet magnitudes while comparing both MFDFA and wavelet-leader/WTMM estimators and ranking measures by cross-well reproducibility.

Remaining gap: The defensible gap is not “compare multifractal methods on well logs”; that would be generic and substantially overlaps Subhakar–Chandrasekhar and Ouadfeul–Aliouane. The precise differentiator must be: a pre-registered measure-definition benchmark in which each well-log property is converted into explicitly specified positive measures (raw-property mass after an auditable positivity rule, absolute increments at stated lags, transformed-property mass with transformation sensitivity analysis, and wavelet coefficient/leader magnitudes), all analyzed on identical depth windows and scaling ranges with MFDFA and wavelet-based estimators, then evaluated by cross-well/intra-well reproducibility, parameter sensitivity, and surrogate null tests. The primary outcome should be a stability/reproducibility criterion and a decision rule for when an apparent multifractal spectrum is measure-induced, estimator-induced, or robustly geological. Reservoir-characterization performance should be secondary validation, not the novelty claim.

Cautious novelty decision: PROVISIONALLY NOVEL, medium confidence, conditional on executing the differentiator above. The literature supports novelty of the integrated measure-selection and reproducibility protocol, but not novelty of multifractal analysis of well logs, WTMM/MFDFA application, surrogate testing, or generic method comparison. Before claiming publication-level novelty, perform citation chaining from Khue (2002), Ouadfeul & Aliouane (2011, 2013), Subhakar & Chandrasekhar (2016), and the Neuman apparent-multifractality papers, and verify whether any recent preprint has already performed the exact factorial comparison of positive measures across multiple wells.
